# The writer's own quotable line

Borrowing a quote transfers someone else's authority. The pull quote builds the writer's own: the
one sentence from the piece that a reader repeats with the writer's name under it. A piece has one.

The line is the writer's. Find it in their words, or ask the questions that produce it. Do not
write it for them unless they ask; tighten only by cutting or reordering their words, and show
before → after.

## Find it in the draft

Read the whole draft. Pull at most three candidate sentences, verbatim, each with where it sits
(section, paragraph). Look where writers usually bury them:

- The last sentence of the strongest section.
- The sentence right after a story or example, where the writer says what it meant.
- A sentence in the middle of a long paragraph that states the piece's claim more sharply than
  the headline does.
- A phrase the writer uses twice without noticing.

## Test it

**Stands alone.** Read it with nothing around it but the writer's name. It fails if it needs the
previous sentence: a "this", "that" or "it" with no referent, a term the piece defined earlier, a
"but" or "so" that leans on what came before.

**Scores on what makes lines quoted.** Quotewise rates quoteworthiness on twelve aspects. Eleven
apply to a sentence; the twelfth, authority, comes from the piece around it. Rate each candidate
1–10, calibrated hard (most sentences are a 5), and report only the strongest two and the weakest
two. No line needs all of them; a pull quote usually wins on three or four.

| Aspect | Question | If weak, ask the writer |
|---|---|---|
| Conciseness | Brief yet complete? | Which words can go without losing the claim? |
| Wisdom | Does it hold a truth or insight? | What did you learn that the reader has not? |
| Memorability | Could the reader repeat it tomorrow? | Say it aloud. Where do you stumble? |
| Universality | Does it apply beyond this case? | Who else is this true for? |
| Paradox | Does it use tension or contrast? | What does it overturn? Put both halves in the sentence |
| Metaphor | Is there an image? | What does this look like, physically? |
| Actionable | Can it guide a decision? | What should the reader do differently on Monday? |
| Emotional | Does it move the reader? | What did it cost you? |
| Timelessness | Will it hold in ten years? | Which word dates it (a tool, a year, a trend)? |
| Craft | Is the phrasing exact? | Which word is approximately right instead of exactly right? |
| Thought-provoking | Does it leave a question open? | What should the reader still be turning over? |
| Authority | Does the speaker's standing strengthen it? | Does the piece show why you are the one to say this? |

**Has the shape.** The same service reads these text signals. Check each; none is a rule.

| Signal | Check |
|---|---|
| Length | One sentence, two at most. 15 words or fewer is ideal, 35 the ceiling. Fits in 280 characters with the writer's name |
| Reading level | Plain words. A reader gets it in one pass |
| Repetition | A word or structure repeated on purpose ("not X but X-er") |
| Negation or contrast | "not", "never", "no": saying what the thing is not sharpens what it is |
| Imperative | Starts with a verb, when the line is advice |
| First person | "I" or "we", when the line is a conviction earned by experience |
| Question | A question, when the piece is built to answer it |

## Has someone already said it?

Run `quotewise:quotes_like(quote="<the sentence>")` before the writer builds a piece around it.
If it reads like a known saying, also `quotewise:who_said`.

| Result | Tell the writer |
|---|---|
| A close match in wording | Who said it, where, with the link. Options: quote and credit them, or say what is different in their own version |
| Same idea, different words | It is theirs to say. Name the nearest two so they know the company the line keeps |
| Nothing close | Say so. This is not proof the line is new, only that the corpus has no near match |

## When there is no line yet

The piece has a claim but no sentence that carries it. Ask, one question at a time, and let the
writer answer in their own words:

- "What one sentence from this would you want repeated with your name under it?"
- "What does the reader believe before this piece, and what after? Say the 'after' in one line."
- "Which common advice on this is wrong? Say the opposite in one sentence."
- "Finish it: 'The thing nobody tells you about ___ is ___.'"
- "What did you say to the person in the story, or wish you had?"

The line is usually inside the answer. Quote it back, then tighten by cutting. If the writer asks
for options, give at most three, built from their words, with any word you added marked so they
can see it.

## Where it goes in the piece

The line has to be stated in the piece, in the same words everywhere it appears.

| Position | Use it when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Closing sentence of the section that proves it | Default. The reader has just been shown why it is true | Do not follow it with a sentence that explains it |
| Last line of the piece | It is the piece's conclusion | It must have appeared once earlier; an ending is a return, not a first statement |
| First line | It is a claim the reader will resist, and the piece then earns it | Return to it at the close, same words |
| A section header | Headers are written as full sentences that carry the argument | A header that is only a label cannot hold it |

- State it once where it is earned and once at the close. Twice, identical. A third time dilutes it.
- Give it its own line or short paragraph. A line buried mid-paragraph does not get quoted.
- If the piece uses emphasis (bold, a styled pull quote), spend it on this line only.
- Reuse it verbatim in the subject line, summary or social post. Paraphrasing it elsewhere makes
  two weaker lines out of one.
- A long piece may have one quotable line per section; still only one is the piece's line. The
  others become the standalone posts when the piece is split up.
- A borrowed quote should not sit next to it. If an epigraph or closer says the same thing better,
  the writer chooses one.

## After publishing

The line is the first standalone post, verbatim, with a link back. Keep the published wording and
date wherever the writer keeps their record of the piece, so the next piece can refer to it
exactly.
